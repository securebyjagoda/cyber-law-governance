# Massachusetts General Laws Chapter 93H (MGL c. 93H)

## Overview

Massachusetts General Laws Chapter 93H is the Commonwealth's primary data breach notification law. It governs how organizations must respond when personal information belonging to Massachusetts residents is accessed, used, disclosed, or acquired without authorization.

While 201 CMR 17.00 establishes the security safeguards organizations should implement to prevent breaches, MGL c. 93H defines what organizations must do when a breach occurs. Together, these laws form the foundation of Massachusetts data protection requirements.

For organizations conducting third-party due diligence, background screening, and vendor assessments, MGL c. 93H plays a critical role because the process often involves collecting and maintaining sensitive personal information that may be subject to breach notification obligations.

---

## Why It Matters

Due diligence processes routinely involve collecting identity information, financial information, background screening information, ownership records, and other sensitive data elements. If this information is compromised, organizations may be legally required to notify regulators and affected individuals.

Because due diligence data is often highly sensitive and centralized within screening systems, a breach involving this information may create significant legal, operational, financial, and reputational consequences.

MGL c. 93H helps ensure that organizations respond appropriately to security incidents while providing transparency to affected individuals whose information may have been exposed.

---

## Scope

MGL c. 93H applies to any person, business, organization, or entity that owns, licenses, stores, maintains, or processes personal information relating to Massachusetts residents.

The law applies regardless of where the organization is physically located. The determining factor is whether information belonging to Massachusetts residents is involved.

Organizations conducting due diligence activities may therefore fall within the scope of the law whenever they collect, store, or maintain personal information as part of vendor screening, contractor screening, investor due diligence, or related risk assessment activities.

---

## Protected Information

The law protects personal information belonging to Massachusetts residents.

Historically, protected information included a resident's first name (or first initial) and last name combined with:

- Social Security Number
- Driver's License Number
- State Identification Card Number
- Financial Account Number
- Credit Card Number
- Debit Card Number

Recent updates have expanded the scope of protected information to include additional sensitive categories such as:

- Biometric Data
- Genetic Information
- Medical Information
- Health Insurance Information
- Geolocation Data
- Usernames combined with passwords or security credentials

Many of these data elements can appear during due diligence and background screening activities, particularly during verification and financial review processes.

---

## Key Requirements

The primary purpose of MGL c. 93H is to establish breach notification obligations.

When a security breach involving protected information occurs, organizations may be required to notify:

- The Massachusetts Attorney General
- The Office of Consumer Affairs and Business Regulation (OCABR)
- Affected Massachusetts residents

Notifications must contain information required by law and be provided without unreasonable delay once the organization determines a reportable breach has occurred.

Additionally, when a breach involves Social Security Numbers, organizations may be required to provide affected individuals with complimentary credit monitoring services for a specified period.

The law therefore creates obligations not only to detect incidents, but also to document, investigate, communicate, and respond to them effectively.

---

## Relationship to Due Diligence

Due diligence processes frequently involve collecting information that falls directly within the scope of MGL c. 93H.

Examples include:

- Social Security Numbers
- Financial Information
- Identity Verification Data
- Personal Contact Information
- Ownership Information
- Consumer Report Data

Because these activities often require organizations to centralize sensitive information from multiple sources, they create significant responsibilities regarding data protection and incident response.

A breach involving due diligence records may trigger notification obligations even when the information was originally collected to satisfy legal, regulatory, or business requirements.

---

## Relationship to Other Laws

MGL c. 93H works closely with several other laws relevant to due diligence activities.

Most notably, it is closely connected to 201 CMR 17.00.

While MGL c. 93H focuses on what organizations must do after a breach occurs, 201 CMR 17.00 establishes the administrative, technical, and physical safeguards intended to prevent breaches from occurring in the first place.

The law also intersects with:

- GLBA, which governs financial information.
- FCRA, which governs consumer reports and background checks.
- MGL c. 214 § 1B, which establishes privacy rights and limits unreasonable interference with personal information.
- GDPR, when organizations process information relating to individuals located within the European Union.

Together, these laws create a broader framework governing the collection, use, protection, and disclosure of sensitive information.

---

## Governance Implications

Organizations handling due diligence information should ensure they maintain governance processes capable of supporting incident response and breach management.

Examples include:

- Written Information Security Program (WISP)
- Incident Response Plan
- Data Retention Schedule
- Vendor Management Program
- Third-Party Risk Assessment Process
- Security Awareness Training Program
- Breach Notification Procedures

These governance practices help organizations prepare for security incidents and demonstrate compliance with regulatory obligations.

---

## Cybersecurity Implications

Compliance with MGL c. 93H requires organizations to develop the ability to detect, investigate, contain, document, and respond to security incidents.

Effective compliance is supported by cybersecurity practices such as:

- Encryption
- Access Control
- Authentication
- Security Monitoring
- Audit Logging
- Data Loss Prevention (DLP)
- Secure Data Disposal
- Incident Detection and Response
- Forensic Investigation Capabilities

These safeguards help reduce the likelihood of reportable breaches and provide evidence needed to support investigations when incidents occur.
