# Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA)

## Overview

The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA), also known as the Financial Services Modernization Act, governs how financial institutions collect, use, share, and protect customers' nonpublic personal information (NPI).

Although GLBA is most commonly associated with banks and traditional financial institutions, it may also apply to organizations operating in the real estate sector when they engage in activities such as lending, loan servicing, debt management, evaluating creditworthiness, processing financial transactions, handling investor funds, or obtaining financial information during due diligence and background screening activities.

For organizations conducting corporate due diligence, GLBA becomes relevant whenever financial information is collected, evaluated, stored, or shared as part of the risk assessment process.

---

## Why It Matters

Financial information is often one of the most valuable and sensitive categories of information collected during due diligence.

Organizations may review financial statements, credit reports, liabilities, investor information, debt obligations, bankruptcy records, and other financial data to assess whether a third party presents an acceptable level of financial risk.

Because this information can reveal significant details about an individual's or organization's finances, unauthorized disclosure can lead to financial harm, fraud, identity theft, regulatory penalties, and reputational damage.

GLBA establishes federal requirements governing both the use and protection of this information and therefore plays a significant role whenever financial due diligence activities occur.

---

## Scope

GLBA applies to financial institutions and organizations engaged in financial activities.

In the real estate industry, organizations may fall within the scope of GLBA when they:

- Evaluate creditworthiness
- Handle investor funds
- Provide lending or loan-servicing activities
- Process financial transactions
- Collect consumer financial information
- Obtain consumer reports as part of a due diligence process

The law applies to both the collection and protection of nonpublic personal information obtained through such activities.

---

## Protected Information

GLBA protects nonpublic personal information (NPI).

Examples of information commonly encountered during due diligence activities include:

- Credit reports
- Financial statements
- Banking information
- Revenue and profitability information
- Debt and liability information
- Investor financial information
- Funding and transaction records
- Consumer financial information

When this information can be linked to an identifiable individual or customer, it falls within GLBA's scope and must be handled in accordance with the Privacy Rule and Safeguards Rule.

---

## Key Requirements

GLBA contains two major components relevant to due diligence activities.

### Privacy Rule

The Privacy Rule governs how organizations collect, use, disclose, and share nonpublic personal information.

Organizations subject to GLBA must:

- Provide Privacy Notices to customers
- Explain how information is used and shared
- Provide required Opt-Out Notices where applicable
- Limit disclosures of protected information unless authorized or permitted by law

### Safeguards Rule

The Safeguards Rule focuses on cybersecurity and information security requirements.

Organizations must develop, implement, and maintain safeguards designed to:

- Ensure the security and confidentiality of customer information
- Protect information against anticipated threats or hazards
- Prevent unauthorized access or use
- Protect against substantial harm or inconvenience to customers

To satisfy these obligations, organizations are generally expected to implement a comprehensive security program that includes administrative, technical, and physical safeguards.

Examples include:

- Encryption
- Access Control
- Authentication Controls
- Security Monitoring
- Employee Training
- Vulnerability Assessments
- Penetration Testing
- Data Retention Procedures

---

## Relationship to Due Diligence

GLBA is most relevant during the Financial Checks phase of the due diligence process.

Organizations frequently collect and analyze financial information to determine:

- Financial stability
- Creditworthiness
- Liquidity
- Bankruptcy history
- Investment risk
- Ability to satisfy contractual obligations

Because this information often contains protected financial data, GLBA governs how the information may be collected, stored, accessed, shared, and ultimately disposed of.

The law therefore directly influences the governance and security requirements associated with financial due diligence activities.

---

## Relationship to Other Laws

GLBA works alongside several other laws commonly encountered during due diligence and background screening processes.

While GLBA determines obligations relating to financial information, Massachusetts regulation 201 CMR 17.00 establishes technical security requirements that apply to personal information belonging to Massachusetts residents.

The two frameworks are highly complementary.

Many safeguards required under GLBA's Safeguards Rule, including access controls, security monitoring, encryption, employee training, and risk management processes, align closely with the security expectations established under 201 CMR 17.00.

Similarly, organizations conducting background checks may also encounter obligations under the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA), which governs consumer reports and credit information. GLBA focuses on protecting financial information, while FCRA focuses on the permissible use and accuracy of consumer reporting data.

Together, these laws create a layered compliance structure governing financial information collected during due diligence.

---

## Governance Implications

Organizations handling financial information as part of due diligence activities should maintain:

- Written Information Security Program (WISP)
- Vendor Management Program
- Third-Party Risk Assessment Process
- Data Retention Schedule
- Records of Processing Activities (ROPA), where applicable
- Security Awareness and Training Program
- Incident Response Plan

These governance mechanisms support both GLBA compliance and broader information-security objectives.

---

## Cybersecurity Implications

GLBA places significant emphasis on the protection of financial information through cybersecurity controls.

Organizations should implement safeguards capable of preserving the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information throughout the due diligence lifecycle.

Common controls include:

- Encryption
- Access Control
- Authentication
- Security Monitoring
- Audit Logging
- Data Loss Prevention (DLP)
- Vulnerability Management
- Secure Data Disposal
- Third-Party Risk Management

These controls help reduce the likelihood of unauthorized access, disclosure, alteration, or loss of protected financial information.
